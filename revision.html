Six Faults to Fix

Everything on this page came off your last three lessons, worked through your instructor's routine so he sees the improvement in the shape he teaches. Five of the six faults are really one fault wearing different clothes: a step of MSPSpG arriving late.

MSPSpG — the spine

Every hazard, junction, bend and parked car runs through these five in this order. Each one finishes before the next starts, and all of them finish before the wheel moves.

M Mirror Interior first, then the door mirror on the side you're moving toward.
S Signal In good time, if anyone at all benefits from knowing.
P Position Settle the line early, while you're still travelling straight.
Sp Speed All braking done here. Nothing to adjust once you're steering.
G Gear Last thing before the turn — chosen to match the speed you've just set.

Gear is last because speed decides it.

You can't pick the gear until you know how fast you'll actually be going, which is why Sp comes before G and why block-changing straight to the right gear works. Get to your speed on the straight, take the gear that suits it, then both hands go on the wheel and stay there. Anything still being adjusted after G is a fault waiting to be written down.

First or second?

If the car will be under about 10 mph at the point you're steering, it's first.

Second gear starts labouring below roughly 10 mph — that shudder, or needing a big stab of gas to keep going, is the car telling you it should have been first. Above 12–15 mph second is comfortable and first will scream. There's no in-between: pick on the approach, not mid-turn.

Changing down — 30 into a 20

  1. Read the sign early. You want to be at 20 by the sign, not slowing past it — start roughly 100 m out.
  2. Mirror, then gas off. Let engine braking take the first few mph before you touch the brake.
  3. Progressive brake. Light, firmer through the middle, then ease the pressure off as you reach 20 so the car settles instead of nodding.
  4. Now the gear. At or just under 20: clutch down, third straight to second, clutch up smoothly, gas on to hold it.

Three that get marked. Taking second while still doing 28 — it jolts, and it reads as braking with the gearbox. Holding the clutch down through the braking — that's coasting. And arriving at the sign still slowing, which is a speed fault however smooth the change was.

Read the column you're in and the gear is already chosen.

Brakes to slow, gears to go — so by the time you reach G you've finished braking and the speed column tells you the answer. Say the gear out loud as you take it; if the number you say doesn't match the speed you're doing, one of the two is wrong and there's still road left to fix it.

The six

Planning & anticipation Marked as: speed — not appropriate to the road

Carrying too much speed into bends

"Planning for bends on road ahead, I went too fast."

What's actually happening

You're reading the bend when you arrive at it instead of on the approach, so the braking happens while you're already turning. An examiner sees the correction — a late brake, a tightened line, a drift toward the centre — rather than the speed itself.

The drill — limit point

  1. Find the furthest point where the two kerbs appear to meet. That's your limit point.
  2. If it's moving away from you, your speed is fine or you can build.
  3. If it's staying still or coming toward you, the bend is tightening — come off the gas and brake now, before the wheel moves.
  4. All braking belongs at Sp, on the straight. Steady gas through the bend, gently on as the limit point opens back up.
Say it "Coming at me — slow. Running away — go."
Clutch control Marked as: control — clutch / roundabouts

Lifting the clutch on a tight uphill roundabout

"I lifted the clutch too high at an uphill roundabout start… I rushed and flew out, giving me less turn time."

What's actually happening

Uphill you need more gas to hold the bite, so when the clutch comes fully up the car gets all of that at once and surges. On a small tight roundabout you've then got a lot of steering to do and no time left to do it. The fault isn't the entry decision — it's that the car got away from you.

The drill

  1. First gear, held at the bite with the footbrake on while you look right.
  2. Gap appears: brake off, gas steady, clutch stays at the bite.
  3. Keep it there through the whole roundabout — you're controlling speed with your left foot, not your right.
  4. Clutch fully up only once the wheel is straightening onto your exit.
Say it "Hold the bite until I'm straight."
Progress Marked as: undue hesitation

Waiting for a gap that's bigger than you need

"Progress too slow, waited too long."

What's actually happening

Two separate things get marked here. At junctions, turning down a gap you could safely have taken. On the open road, sitting at 24 in a 30 or 45 in a 60 when it's clear and dry — the limit is the target when conditions allow, not a ceiling to stay well under.

The drill

  • Junctions: if the nearest approaching car is more than about four seconds away, that's your gap. Finish G early and sit at the bite, ready — most "too slow" faults are really "wasn't prepared to move".
  • Open road: every time the limit changes, say the new number out loud and get to it within a few seconds unless something stops you.
  • If you genuinely can't go, keep creeping so it reads as assessing, not freezing.
Say it "Safe, not perfect. Take it."
Positioning Marked as: normal driving position

Sitting too far from the kerb

"Sometimes I'm too wide on road, should be keeping more left and following kerb."

What's actually happening

Normal position is roughly a metre out from the kerb — about a door's width. Wider than that and you're crowding the centre line, which invites oncoming traffic to flinch and makes every right-hand bend look worse than it is. It usually creeps in when you're concentrating on something else, so it's a checking habit rather than a steering problem.

The drill — set a reference point

  1. Parked correctly about a metre from the kerb, note where the kerb line crosses your bonnet or windscreen from your seat.
  2. That mark is your reference for the rest of your driving — same seat position every time.
  3. On the move, glance at it on every straight. Correct with a small steady input, not a jerk back in.
  4. Widen deliberately only for parked cars, cyclists and blind left-hand bends — then return.
Say it "A door's width off the kerb."
Signals Marked as: signals — necessary / correctly

No signal when passing a parked car

"I can forget to signal when overtaking a parked car blocking the road."

What's actually happening

Pulling out around an obstruction is a change of course, so the full routine applies — it isn't a lesser manoeuvre than a junction. Signal if it would help anyone: traffic behind, oncoming, or a pedestrian waiting to cross. Because you're focused on the gap ahead you jump straight to P, and the M and S that should come first get dropped. The missing mirror is usually the bigger fault of the two.

The drill — the full routine, every car

  1. Mirror — interior, then right.
  2. Signal right if anyone benefits. Default to signalling here; it's almost never wrong.
  3. Position — out early and smoothly, a door's width clear of the parked car.
  4. Speed — and if the obstruction is on your side, priority is oncoming: be ready to hold back.
  5. Gear — second if you're squeezing through slowly, and look through the gaps for feet, doors and turning wheels as you go.
Say it "Obstruction ahead — mirror, signal, out."
Gears Marked as: control — gears / junctions

Knowing when the turn needs first

"One of my main weaknesses is knowing when to do 1st gear turns."

Make it a decision, not a guess

By the time you reach G you've already set your speed, so ask one question: can I see it's clear, and can I keep rolling? Yes is second. No — you'll have to stop, creep, or crawl round something tight — is first. The table above is that question already answered for the twelve situations you'll meet on test.

The drill

  • Commentary for a whole lesson: name the gear out loud when you first see the junction, then check whether you were right by the time you reach G.
  • Being in first and not needing it costs you nothing. Being in second and needing first costs you a stall or a lurch.
  • If you're unsure and already below walking-jog pace — take first.
Say it "Stopping or crawling? First."

Five of the six come back to the same thing: a step of the routine arriving late. Mirror, signal, position, speed and gear all want to be finished while the road ahead is still straight — by the time you're steering, your only job is steering. Pick one cue per lesson and commentate it out loud so your instructor can hear the thinking, not just see the result; that moves faster than trying to hold all six at once.
